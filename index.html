    For grown-ups: what this practices
    Each stop offers three levels — Preschool (ages 3–4), Kindergarten (age 5), and 1st Grade (age 6) — drawn from the Utah Core Standards for Early Learning and the Kindergarten and 1st Grade Cores. A round is 6 questions at whichever level is selected; every completed round earns at least one star, so nothing here is a "fail" — missed answers just get another gentle try.

    Why the stops are in this order. The National Reading Panel's "five pillars" of reading instruction build in a sequence: phonemic/phonological awareness (hearing and playing with sounds), then phonics (connecting those sounds to letters), then fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension (using both to read and understand real text). So Rhyme River (sound play) now comes before ABC Trailhead (letter-sound matching), which comes before the new Story Cove stop — where preschoolers match a spoken phrase to a picture, kindergartners read one decodable sentence and answer a question about it, and 1st graders read a short two-sentence passage and answer a comprehension question. That's the actual point of the earlier phonics work, so it gets its own stop instead of stopping at single letters and words.

    Why some letters wait. Reading researcher Tim Shanahan notes there's no single research-proven "best" order for teaching letters — but there is broad agreement on a few things: teach frequent letters before rare ones, mix consonants and vowels early, and keep visually or phonetically similar letters (like b and d) apart at first. The Preschool letter-matching game now uses a curated set of ten visually distinct letters and holds "D" back so it isn't confused with "B" on a child's very first pass; both appear together once matching moves to sounds at Kindergarten.

    Color Canyon. Utah's Core Standards for Early Learning list "recognize and name multiple colors" as a Fine Arts skill for 4-year-olds, so it earned its own stop. Preschool is pure color recognition (matching a color swatch, no words to lean on); Kindergarten connects a color name to a real object (an apple is red, a blueberry is blue); 1st Grade moves into basic color mixing — red and blue make purple, blue and yellow make green, red and yellow make orange.
